## 2. Andalusian Cuisine

### 2.1 Historical Roots of Andalusian Cuisine: A Journey Through Time (Version 1)
- Moorish invasion: 711. Arabic rule lasted nearly eight centuries.
- Introduced: cumin, saffron, cinnamon, almonds, honey.
- Historian Dr. Fernanda Gómez discovered an ancient Arab cookbook in 2023.
- Dishes with Arab roots: 'Pollo al Ajillo' (garlic chicken) and 'Jamoncillo de Pinones' (pine nut sweets), first mentioned in records from the 12th century.
- Columbus sailed in 1492 from the port of Palos de la Frontera. The Columbian Exchange brought tomatoes, potatoes, peppers and beans.
- Gazpacho was originally bread, water, vinegar and garlic; tomatoes and bell peppers were added later.
- Chef José Rodríguez of Seville's restaurant "Raíces" (October 15, 2023) emphasised innovation alongside respect for historical recipes.
- Jewish Andalusians, before their 1492 expulsion, made 'adafina', a slow-cooked Sabbath stew of meat, chickpeas and spices.
- Chef Elena Suárez recreated a 14th-century adafina recipe December 8, 2023.
- 'Pan de Cádiz' is a marzipan confection with candied fruit, linked to Jewish pastry traditions.
- Reconquista: pork returned to the diet. Jamón Ibérico, chorizo and 'cochinillo asado' (roast suckling pig) became Christian symbols.
- Historian Marta Vargas lectured on pork's cultural role September 4, 2023.
- Convents and monasteries (16th–18th century): 'Yemas de San Leandro' and 'Tocino de cielo' were made from surplus egg yolks left after clarifying wine with egg whites.
- 19th-century landowner cuisine: 'Rabo de Toro' (oxtail stew) and 'Perdiz estofada' (partridge stew). The Duke of Medinaceli hosted grand banquets.
- Chef Carlos García opened "Sabor Ancestral" January 5, 2024. His reinvented 'Pescaito Frito' (fried fish) dates to Phoenician times.

### 2.2 Historical Roots of Andalusian Cuisine: A Journey Through Time (Version 2)
- Phoenicians arrived in the 11th century BC, introducing olives and wine. Romans followed with agriculture.
- Moors brought irrigation, rice, citrus, cumin and saffron in the 8th century AD.
- Gazpacho's Roman origin: bread pounded with olive oil and garlic. Moors added vinegar. New World tomatoes and peppers followed after 1492.
- Tapas legend: during the 13th-century reign of Alfonso X ("The Wise King"), ill health limited him to small food portions with wine, supposedly originating the custom.
- Golden Age (16th–17th centuries): New World foods (potatoes, tomatoes, peppers, chocolate) entered Andalusian kitchens.
- Anonymous "Libro de cozina" (late 16th century) mentions rabo de toro and chocos (cuttlefish).
- Rural dishes: migas (fried breadcrumbs with chorizo) and puchero (meat and vegetable stew).
- Spanish Civil War and dictatorship brought rationing. Democracy returned in 1975.
- Chef Dani García is cited as an avant-garde reinterpreter.
- Andalusian Culinary Heritage Symposium, third annual, held in Málaga November 17, 2023.
- Present-day dishes: pescaíto frito (coastal) and morcilla (blood sausage, mountains).

### 2.3 The Andalusian Dessert Palette: Sweet Endings from Southern Spain
- Moorish-era sweets: almonds, honey, sugar. Pestiños (honey-glazed fritters) were originally made in convents.
- Carmen Ruiz, a Málaga pastry chef, demonstrated pestiños at the Virgen de la Victoria festival September 8, 2023.
- Almond desserts: turrón, bienmesabe. Citrus zest: tortas de aceite.
- Pastry historian Juan García (Seville) spoke October 27, 2023, on eggs in Andalusian sweets. Tocino de cielo means "bacon from heaven."
- María López's Granada workshop presented alfajores December 12, 2023.
- Roscos de vino (aniseed and wine doughnuts) are Christmas treats. Antonio Fernández of Jerez spoke February 1, 2024.
- Luisa Jiménez (Córdoba) deconstructed leche frita (fried milk), April 5, 2024.
- Andalusian Culinary Institute courses since September 2023; director José Paredes.
- Ana Gómez showcased organic almond flour at a Málaga fair September 15, 2024.
- Feria de Abril in Seville features mantecados and polvorones. Bizcocho borracho (tipsy cake) and arroz con leche are also named.

## 41. Kagoshima and Satsuma-age

### 41.1 History and Origins of Dishes: Investigating Tradition
- Chef Hiroshi Sato of Kagoshima, 72, fourth generation; breakfast October 3, 2023, at his restaurant overlooking Sakurajima.
- Satsuma-age: fish cake; fishermen minced excess catch, mixed with salt, formed and fried it to extend shelf life and portability.
- Roots in the Satsuma Domain, contacts with the Ryukyu Kingdom and mainland China.
- Sato cites September 10, 1550, when Korean craftsmen's techniques merged with Satsuma methods (per Sato).
- Sweet potatoes introduced in the 17th century to combat famine.
- Meiji period (1868–1912) Western ingredients changed preparation.
- Sato: "Cooking connects us to history."
- Other dishes cited: Moroccan tagine (Berber culture, 9th-century pottery), Neapolitan pizza (tomatoes from the Americas, 16th century), Eastern European borscht.

## 42. Gyutan (Sendai, Japan)

### 42.1 Unearthing Sendai's Gyutan Story
- Gyutan (grilled beef tongue) traced to Sendai shortly after World War II.
- Chef Keishiro Sano, yakitori restaurant owner, text dates his beef venture to September 9, 1948 (during American occupation).
- He marinated tongue for hours and grilled it, served with barley rice and tail soup.
- Spread regionally by the late 1950s; nationally afterwards.
- 1980s economic boom brought international attention.
- After 2000, gyutan entered international menus.
- Masaru Kamikura (Tokyo, October 2023) introduced a deconstructed version.
- Sendai Gyutan Festival, 65th anniversary, August 5, 2024.
- Miyagi Culinary Arts Academy teaches gyutan.
