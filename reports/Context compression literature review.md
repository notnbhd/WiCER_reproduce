# Literature backs verbatim, coherent compiled context

The 2024–2026 literature supports TACT's main result. Among the methods that can run before the question is known, the one that keeps working is **extractive, sentence-granular, order-preserving selection of verbatim source text**. Abstractive summaries lose answer-critical details ([Jha et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08892)), token pruning breaks grammar and comprehension, and converting text to triples removes about a third of answer entities and makes the reader abstain ([Han et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11371v3)). Each of our three findings has a published counterpart, but none has been tested in our exact setting. **No paper we found compares claim triples with verbatim sentences as a fixed-budget, preloaded reader context. No compression paper measures anaphora breakage or abstention as an outcome. Nothing evaluates query-agnostic compression on RepLiQA.** Those are the openings where our results are new. Two lines of evidence change the plan for next experiments. First, KV-cache quantization is the most robust compressor in shared-cache, many-question settings ([SCBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.10319)). llama.cpp's q8_0-K / q4_0-V costs very little perplexity ([PR #7412](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/7412)), so fitting the full source may beat any text cut. Second, an October 2026 paper finds that sentence selection tuned to particular questions does no better than random deletion on later questions, while LLMLingua-2 beats random by 12.5 points ([Yao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27301)). Random-sentence and LLMLingua-2 baselines are therefore required before any claim about question-guided selection. On abstention, models respond to topically relevant but insufficient context by answering rather than abstaining, and they cannot see gaps that leave no marker ([Sufficient Context](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.06037), [AbsenceBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.11440v1)). That explains our unanswerable regression and points to omission markers as the cheapest fix to test.

**How to read the verification markers.** The research notes used different conventions; this report maps them to three markers. **[V]**: the arXiv or venue page was checked during the research session (title, authors, ID). **[M]**: cited from memory or prior knowledge; the ID and any numbers were not re-checked, so spot-check before citing. **[U]**: the paper is verified, but a specific detail is not, usually the venue or a number taken from a search snippet or third-party summary. The table notes say which detail. One conflict between notes was resolved in favour of the more thorough check. The CAG note marked Jha et al.'s extractive-vs-abstractive result as recollection, but the text-compression note read the full HTML and confirmed it, so it is [V] here.

**What "our findings" means in the relevance columns.** (F1) Verbatim source sentences beat typed claim/triple layouts: rendering the same selection verbatim scores +0.56 over truncation on a 1–5 judge scale, pooled over 3 topics. (F2) Coherent contiguous text beats max-coverage selection at a ~45% budget. (F3) Fragmented context hurts unanswerable accuracy. The notes describe two directions here, and they should be kept apart: sentence-selected context makes the reader *answer* questions it should mark "not mentioned", and the triple layout makes it *over-abstain* on answerable questions.

## Question-blind compression works only when it extracts whole sentences

The theory sets the ceiling first. A rate-distortion analysis of hard-prompt compression finds that query-aware compression fundamentally beats query-agnostic compression, and that current methods sit far from the optimum. Only a query-aware, variable-rate LLMLingua-2 variant beats the *optimal* query-agnostic strategy ([Nagle et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.15504)). For a compiler that never sees the question, the remaining room is therefore in **variable-rate allocation**, spending the budget unevenly across documents and sections, more than in a better per-token scorer.

The empirical comparisons agree on how methods rank. On LongBench, extractive reranker-based compression "often outperforms all the other approaches", including gains such as **+7.89 on 2WikiMQA at 7.75x**. Question-blind abstractive summaries trail extraction by **10–15 points on MultiFieldQA**, and LongLLMLingua-style token pruning is usually worst; the authors blame "the disruption of grammar and sentence comprehension caused by unstructured pruning" ([Jha et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08892)). A six-method study finds that every compressor raises hallucination, mostly through information loss. In its appendix, Claude-3-Haiku itself calls a compressed prompt "incomplete and grammatically flawed" ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00019)). The closest 2026 benchmark to our "questions unknown" setting, GACC-Bench, gives the compressor a goal but not the questions. At 5% retention, all three LLMLingua-family pruners score 0 macro accuracy. Even at 10% retention, the best LLM compressors reach only ~57%, against 99.4% with full context ([GACC-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21893)). Our 45% budget is much milder. Both Zhang et al. and Jha et al. find that compression around 2x costs little or even helps, which matches the recovery we measured.

There is one useful tension with F2. Jha et al. found that at *high* compression, larger chunks do worse because fewer of them fit, so coverage beats coherence there. Our coherence-over-coverage result at ~2.2x may reverse at harsher budgets. A budget sweep would show where it flips.

## Quantizing the KV cache could replace the text cut entirely

KV quantization keeps every token and loses only numerical precision. It is therefore query-agnostic by construction and holds up when one cache is reused across many questions. On Llama-3-8B-Instruct, **KIVI-4bit (3.11x) scores 45.3 on LongBench against a 45.2 baseline with 100% needle accuracy**. KIVI-2bit (5.05x) still scores 44.3 / 100. LLMLingua-2 at 4x falls to **29.9 LongBench and 8.3 needle** ([Yuan et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01527v2)). SCBench, the closest benchmark to our shared-corpus setting, shows query-aware eviction collapsing under multi-request reuse: **Qwen2.5-72B with SnapKV at 1/32 drops from 45.8 to 3.3**, while KIVI holds 35.8. SnapKV run without the query scores 0.0 on first-query string retrieval ([SCBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10319v2)). KVzip reports that query-aware methods degrade even at a 90% budget once the cache is reused ([KVzip](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23416)).

The numbers that matter for us come from llama.cpp, and they are thinner. PR #7412 measured **q8_0/q8_0 at ΔPPL 0.0046 (KLD 0.00098)** and **q8_0-K / q4_0-V at ΔPPL 0.024 (KLD 0.0051)**, but **q4_0-K costs about ten times more KLD (0.0329)**. K is much more sensitive than V ([llama.cpp PR #7412](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/7412)). KIVI's per-channel key quantization explains why ([KIVI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02750)). Going from 45% to 100% of the source needs about 2.2x more room. q8_0/q8_0 (~1.9x) almost covers that, and q8_0-K / q4_0-V (~2.5x) covers it. The caveat is serious. PR #7412 reports short-context perplexity on an unnamed model, and **no primary source gives long-context QA numbers for llama.cpp's q8_0/q4_0**. The lossless 2–4-bit results from KIVI, KVQuant and TurboQuant rely on per-channel, pre-RoPE and outlier handling that llama.cpp's block q4_0 does not have, so they do not transfer.

Query-agnostic *eviction* (KVzip, Compactor, Q-Filters, Expected Attention) goes further: KVzip keeps 30% of the cache with negligible loss and stacks with 4-bit KV to shrink a 124K-token cache from 16.3 GB to 1.2 GB. None of these methods runs in llama.cpp, though. Eviction also drops content at the token level, so it shares our text cut's risk of losing details.

## Every representation that works keeps the source sentence

The CAG anchor is thin. Chan et al. preload the whole corpus into a KV cache and match or beat RAG *when it fits* ([Chan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15605)). WiCER reports full-context KV inference at **4.38 vs 4.08 for RAG** on 17 RepLiQA domains, blind compilation collapsing to **2.14–2.32**, and probe-guided refinement recovering about 80% ([WiCER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07068)). An arXiv abstract search for "cache-augmented generation" returned only four hits, so the question of *what to put in the cache when the corpus does not fit* is mostly open. The LC-vs-RAG literature settles on "it depends" ([LaRA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09977)). Two of its findings explain our results directly. **Context length alone degrades reading by 13.9–85% even with perfect retrieval** ([Du et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05381)), which is why a compiled context can beat raw full context. **Accuracy collapses when the question and the evidence share no literal tokens** ([NoLiMa](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05167)). Verbatim sentences keep the lexical overlap RepLiQA questions have with the source, and triples or paraphrases remove it. That is the most plausible mechanism for the +0.56.

The triples evidence is unusually direct. In a controlled comparison, **triples-only GraphRAG scores 34.3 F1 on NQ against 64.8 for chunk RAG**. Only ~65% of answer entities make it into the KG, the triples-only system answers "Insufficient Information" where RAG answers, and adding source text back recovers part of the gap ([Han et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11371v3)). That one paper reproduces all three of our failure modes: information loss, reader abstention, and detail questions suffering. TCR-QF restores each triple's source sentence and gains **+29.1% EM** ([TCR-QF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.15378)), the closest published analogue to our "same selection, rendered verbatim" fix. HippoRAG 2 concedes that KG-RAG falls "considerably below standard RAG" on basic factual memory and repairs it by adding passages as graph nodes ([HippoRAG 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14802)). A 2026 chunking reproduction finds **proposition segmentation consistently worst, 15–27% below paragraph chunking**, because the units lose topical context ([Zhou et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16974)). In memory and summary systems the pattern repeats: RAPTOR, MemoRAG, ReadAgent and LongMemEval all keep a path back to verbatim text and use facts as *keys*, not *values*.

The counter-evidence is real but narrow. Dense X propositions and factual decomposition improve QA at small retrieval budgets ([Dense X](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06648), [Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19574)), but those units are self-contained natural-language sentences, not triples, and the gains are on the retrieval side. RAPTOR-style summaries match long context in one evaluation ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01880)), but they are retrieved *alongside* the leaves rather than replacing them. When the knowledge *is* a KG, linearized triples beat verbalized text ([Dai et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11541)). Our loss therefore comes from *extraction* dropping facts and connectives, not from triples being unreadable as a format. That distinction belongs in the write-up.

## Synthetic questions help only with coverage control and honest baselines

No published method keeps verbatim sentences *because* a pool of generated questions is answered by them. That is our planned recipe, and it is open ground. The neighbours either simulate the query at inference time ([LAQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20334), [SpecKV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08373)) or use a query-free proxy such as one generated task descriptor ([TPC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13374)). The most important caution comes from October 2026. On MultiRC, keeping the sentences an LLM judges necessary gains **+11.4 points on the current question** at 25% of tokens. **On later questions, no current-question sentence selector beats random sentence deletion**, and question-agnostic LLMLingua-2 beats random by **12.5 points**, partly because it keeps about 2x as many content words ([Yao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27301)). On Evidence Inference, sentence selection does win. This partly cuts against F2 and F3, and it is exactly what a random-sentence baseline at equal budget would detect. It also conflicts with the KV benchmarks, where LLMLingua-2 does poorly on retrieval-style questions ([Yuan et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01527v2)). Only our own measurement on RepLiQA can settle which effect dominates.

Generated questions are also not a neutral sample of what a document can answer. LLM questions spread their focus evenly and ask for longer answers, while human benchmark questions show positional bias ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03491)). Self-generated QA "saturates early", concentrates on salient spans, and even diverse prompts converge on the same regions. Tying each question to a fixed target span reduces the bias ([Self-Study Reconsidered](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.32002)). Topic- and keyword-seeded generation beats unconstrained generation for document expansion ([Doc2Query++](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09557)). Asking for "20 questions per document" will therefore cluster unless generation is seeded by position or topic.

The closest competitor trains rather than selects. Cartridges builds a per-corpus KV cache offline from self-generated conversations and **matches in-context learning at 38.6x less memory** ([Cartridges](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06266)). EntiGraph, Knowledge Modules, Active Reading and Doc-to-LoRA all find that synthetic, question-shaped exposure beats raw text. All of them need white-box training, and they are weak exactly where RepLiQA probes: Doc-to-LoRA falls from **68% to 16%** as the pretraining prior on a fact gets stronger ([Override Gap](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23750)). A verbatim text extract avoids that failure. On RepLiQA itself, question-conditioned extracts already reach **95% correctness vs 94% for vanilla RAG** ([Highlight & Summarize](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02872)). That is a rough ceiling for "extract, then answer" when the question is known, and the distance from it measures what pre-selection costs.

## Near-miss context and invisible gaps explain the unanswerable drop

Our unanswerable regression follows a documented pattern. Strong readers given topically relevant but insufficient context answer wrongly instead of abstaining, and in one analysis adding context *reduced* abstention. A sufficiency autorater with selective generation improves correct-among-answered by **2–10%** ([Sufficient Context](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.06037)). Twelve generators that were told to abstain still answered **40.0–99.3%** of insufficient-evidence questions ([RINSE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37469)). Small frozen models abstain reliably when evidence is *absent* but answer **41.6%** of misleading-context questions despite abstention prompts ([Setiawan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22228)). Sentence selection maximizes topical relevance, so on unanswerable RepLiQA items it produces exactly this near-miss context. A contiguous prefix more often contains nothing on-topic, and the reader abstains.

The second mechanism is that gaps are invisible. AbsenceBench shows models are poor at noticing removed content. **Claude-3.7-Sonnet reaches ~69.6% F1 at ~5K tokens, and explicit placeholders at omissions improve detection by 35.7%**; the authors attribute this to the fact that gaps have no tokens to attend to ([AbsenceBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.11440v1)). A sentence-selected context hides where it was cut. The closest paper to our setting shows that compression which removes answer evidence produces *hallucination*, not abstention. A 10.1M-parameter LoRA cuts hallucinations by **97%**, while prompt-only abstention over-refuses at high retention ([Compression-Aware Abstention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29934)). That predicts that simply strengthening our "say not mentioned" instruction will trade the unanswerable gain for answerable loss. The over-refusal literature covers the opposite direction, our triple layout over-abstaining: readers refuse answerable questions when the context looks irrelevant or off-distribution ([Zhou et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.01476), [RefusalBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.10390)). That a terse triple layout lowers the reader's sense of sufficiency is our inference; no paper tests it.

## Six experiments the literature points to

The first experiment is the cheapest and could make text compression unnecessary. **Fit the full source with `-ctk q8_0 -ctv q4_0 -fa`**, and if it fits, also try q8_0/q8_0. Compare against the current 45% text with an f16 cache, on held-out questions, over several compile seeds, because compile noise is at least as large as our effects. The supporting evidence is short-context perplexity ([PR #7412](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/7412)) plus 4-bit KV results from schemes better than llama.cpp's ([Yuan et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01527v2)). Do not use q4_0 on K. A positive result is itself publishable, because no source measures llama.cpp KV quantization on long-context QA.

Second, **add random-sentence and LLMLingua-2 baselines at exactly the same token budget**, next to truncation and the verbatim render. Yao et al. show that question-guided sentence selection can tie random deletion and lose to LLMLingua-2 on unseen questions ([Yao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27301)). Our +0.56 over truncation does not yet rule that out. Random sentences also isolate coherence: if random-but-ordered sentences beat max-coverage selection, F2 is about discourse, not about which content was chosen.

Third, **test omission markers**. Insert "[...]" between non-adjacent selected sentences and frame the context as excerpts. Report unanswerable accuracy *and* answerable accuracy, because prompt-side abstention fixes tend to over-refuse ([AbsenceBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.11440v1), [Compression-Aware Abstention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29934)). The measured +35.7% is on omission detection, not on QA, so this is a real test, not a replication. A second arm is a separate sufficiency call before answering ([Sufficient Context](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.06037)).

Fourth, **if we generate questions to guide selection, control their coverage**. Assign each question a target sentence or section before generating ([Self-Study Reconsidered](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.32002), [Doc2Query++](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09557)). Then measure the fraction of RepLiQA gold-answer sentences that the generated questions hit. That costs almost nothing and tells us whether the questions resemble the benchmark's at all ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03491)).

Fifth, **repair anaphora in kept sentences** with minimal bracketed antecedent substitutions, not rewrites. Full rewrites risk Haiku's tendency to paraphrase. The motivation is coreference-in-RAG gains, which are larger for smaller readers ([Jang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07847)), and molecular facts ([Gunjal & Durrett](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.20079)).

Sixth, keeping things lean: allocate **variable per-document budgets** rather than a flat 45% ([Nagle et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.15504), [Compactor](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08143)). Add an entity-preservation count as a judge-free diagnostic ([Łajewska et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19114)), and score the judge on concrete criteria, since holistic LLM-judge ratings disagree badly ([Cochran](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.18490)).

## Where our results would be new

The notes found the same gaps from five directions. **No paper tests claim triples vs verbatim sentences as a fixed-budget preloaded context.** Han et al., TCR-QF and HippoRAG 2 are all retrieval pipelines, so F1 in the CAG setting is new. **No compression paper reports abstention or anaphora breakage as an outcome**, and no study compares contiguous truncation with extractive sentence selection on unanswerable questions, so F3 is unreported. **No benchmark isolates discourse coherence** (ordered vs shuffled vs decontextualized sentences) as a variable for QA or abstention, which leaves F2 open. **No paper evaluates query-agnostic text compression, KV compression, or Cartridges-style self-study on RepLiQA**, despite ~35 citers. **No paper evaluates a pooled-synthetic-question verbatim extract**, the planned recipe. **No benchmark compares LLM-compiled text against KV quantization at matched memory**, and no primary source gives long-context QA numbers for llama.cpp's q8_0/q4_0. Some leads went unchecked: QEvict ([2608.05326](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.05326)) and AnchorKV ([2608.02901](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.02901)), matched-byte KV comparisons seen only as titles [U], plus Stochastic KV Routing ([2604.22782](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22782)), a RepLiQA citer that was not examined and might run KV compression on it.

Before citing anything externally, re-check the [M] entries (the LLMLingua family, RECOMP, Selective Context, CompAct, Power of Noise, Lost in the Middle) and the [U] numbers. Those are TurboQuant's LongBench table, FaithEval's 76.8→7.4, the >55% over-refusal figure, the 15–27% proposition figure, and the LLM-Wiki ablation. Also check the venues for LaRA, Sufficient Context, AbstentionBench, Cartridges, SEAL and Cite Pretrain, and the author order on the rate-distortion paper.

## Conclusion

The literature moves our result from "a surprising win for plain text" to "the expected outcome, in a setting nobody has measured". Every successful representation, whether in GraphRAG, memory systems or prompt compression, keeps a path back to the verbatim sentence. NoLiMa's lexical-overlap result gives a concrete reason why that matters most on RepLiQA-style lookup. The novelty claim is therefore defensible but narrow: it rests on the CAG setting, the fixed budget, the RepLiQA unanswerable slice, and treating coherence and abstention as measured outcomes rather than side effects.

The bigger strategic shift concerns the premise of compressing at all. If q8_0-K / q4_0-V lets the full source fit with little loss, the most interesting question becomes when compiled text *beats* the full source. Du et al.'s length-dilution result says that can happen. KV quantization handles fitting the source; the compiler would earn its place by improving what the reader attends to. Yao et al.'s random-deletion result is the main threat to that story, so the equal-budget random and LLMLingua-2 baselines should run before anything else gets built on question-guided selection.
