## Cultural Landscapes: The Symbiosis of Nature and Human Activity

[cut 34.1]
In the realm of cultural heritage, the concept of cultural landscapes has emerged as a vital framework for understanding the intricate and ongoing relationship between humans and their natural environment.
[cut 34.2]
Cultural landscapes are, by definition, the combined works of nature and of man.
[cut 34.3]
On October 3, 2023, at the annual conference of the International Association for Landscape Ecology, Professor Eliza Reed provided a keynote speech that addressed the genesis of cultural landscapes.
[cut 34.4]
Cultural landscapes are not monolithic. UNESCO classifies them into three main types: clearly defined landscapes designed and created by man, organically evolved landscapes, and associative cultural landscapes.
[cut 34.5]
Artificially designed landscapes are deliberately conceived and created by human designers and artists to fulfill aesthetic reasons. These landscapes are often associated with monumental constructions, gardens, and parks. For instance, on September 15, 2023, landscape architect Helena Suarez received acclaim for her designs of the New Millennium Gardens in Lisbon, which were constructed to commemorate 21st-century peace and environmental conservation through thoughtful, eco-conscious design.
[cut 34.6]
The organically evolved landscapes result from an initial social, economic, cultural, and administrative imperative that has developed their present form by association with, and in
response to, its natural environment.
[cut 34.7]
A notable example is the vineyard landscapes of the Douro Valley in Portugal, where visitors on September 8, 2023, had the opportunity to see the centuries-old wine production practices that have shaped the valley's topography.
[cut 34.8]
Associative cultural landscapes are significant due to the powerful religious, artistic, or cultural associations of the natural element rather than material cultural evidence, which may be insignificant or even absent. A fascinating instance of this is the role of Mount Fuji in Japan, which influences Japanese art and spirituality despite the modest number of physical structures that are located on it.
[cut 34.9]
Socio-economic changes have immense implications for cultural landscapes.
[cut 34.10]
On December 1, 2023, economist Dr. Roberta Kwong delivered a lecture at the Global Forum on Cultural Landscapes elaborating on how shifts in economic patterns, such as the rise of industrial agriculture or the decline of certain forms of craftsmanship, can profoundly affect cultural landscapes.
[cut 34.11]
On November 20, 2023, the World Heritage Committee adopted a new resolution that emphasizes a balanced approach to conservation that respects the living nature of cultural landscapes while protecting their heritage values.
[cut 34.12]
A case in point is the Restoration Project of the Old Terraces in Ifugao, Philippines, where a partnership between government and indigenous populations is working to restore rice terraces without disrupting the centuriesold techniques unique to that landscape.
[cut 34.13]
Climate change, globalization, and urbanization exert unprecedented pressures on the delicate balance of nature and culture.
[cut 34.14]

## Document 80

Bridging Gaps: Intergenerational Art Programs in Our Community Art education is a vital thread in the cultural fabric of any society, fostering creativity and a shared sense of identity among its participants. Intergenerational art programs, which bring together people of all ages to learn from and create with one another, have been gaining traction in communities across the country. These initiatives aim to bridge the gap between generations, encourage mutual respect, and facilitate the transfer of knowledge and skills. In this critical review, we examine the effectiveness of current intergenerational art programs in our community and consider potential improvements that could be made. Roots of Intergenerational Engagement Conceptually, intergenerational programs are not new.
[cut 80.1]
Formal programs that blend these age groups in a learning environment are, however, a relatively recent development. In our community, several such initiatives have taken root since their widespread inception in early September 2023. Visionaries like Martha Sullivan and James Huerta have been at the forefront, using art as a means to unite and heal generational divides. The Palette of Programs From September 15, 2023, we have seen a plethora of projects emerge. Sullivan's brainchild, the "Canvas Bridges" workshop, has been popular for its painting classes, while Huerta's "Rhythms of the Ages" merges storytelling with dance. Both leaders have backgrounds in art therapy and emphasize the importance of a safe and expressive environment for their participants. The programs have shown an incline in attendance and have been lauded for their unique approaches. The artwork produced has even made its way into local exhibitions, notably the "Generations Gallery," which opened to the public on November 3, 2023.
[cut 80.2]
In interviewing participants, we find that many seniors feel a renewed sense of purpose and are invigorated by the fresh perspectives offered by younger individuals. On the flip side, youth express a deeper appreciation for history and life experiences shared by their older counterparts. A study conducted by the local university’s Sociology Department, released on December 15, 2023, indicated improved mental well-being among participants of all ages.
[cut 80.3]
While initiatives like "Canvas Bridges" and "Rhythms of the Ages" have made strides in painting and dance, there is room for expansion into other art forms.
[cut 80.4]
Notably, on February 20, 2024, the Community Arts Foundation announced a strategic plan to create a dedicated funding stream, acknowledging the need for fiscal reliability.
