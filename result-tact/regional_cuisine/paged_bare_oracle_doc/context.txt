Following the Moorish invasion in 711, a multitude of ingredients and techniques were introduced that profoundly affected local cuisine. The year 2023 marked the significant discovery of an ancient Arab cookbook by historian Dr. Fernanda Gómez, providing new insights into medieval Andalusian cooking. [cut 43.1]

[cut 43.2]
From the Phoenicians in the 11th century BC, who introduced the cultivation of olives and wine, to the Romans, who followed suit with their own agricultural practices, Andalusia’s early gastronomic profile was one of simple, hearty fare designed to feed its agrarian societies.
[cut 43.3]
The Moors crossed into Iberia, bringing with them advanced irrigation techniques, new crops like rice and citrus fruits, and a palette of exotic spices like cumin and saffron. This Arabic infusion into local diets laid the foundation of what we now broadly label as Mediterranean cuisine.
[cut 43.4]
Fast forward to the late 15th century, after the Reconquista, when the Christian forces reclaimed Andalusia from Moorish rule.
[cut 43.5]

Another dish, the ubiquitous Andalusian tapas, sprang from both necessity and innovation. There’s the traditional story that, during the 13th-century rule of Alfonso X, 'The Wise King,' ill health dictated that he could only consume small amounts of food with wine between meals, ultimately leading to the custom of tapas.
[cut 43.6]
The 16th and 17th centuries, often referred to as Spain’s Golden Age, saw Andalusian cuisine reach new heights of sophistication. The influx of wealth from the Americas brought previously unknown foodstuffs such as potatoes, tomatoes, bell peppers, and chocolate, which were gradually integrated into local dishes. This amalgamation reached the aristocratic tables of Seville and Cádiz, where epic feasts boasted artful displays of the region's abundance. It was during this period that meat dishes like rabo de toro (oxtail stew) and chocos (cuttlefish) began to appear more frequently in cookbooks, like the anonymously authored "Libro de cozina" of the late 16th century.
[cut 43.7]
